Reading Passage 1

The orchard at the centre of the world

AAlmaty, the largest city in Kazakhstan, sits at the foot of the Tian Shan mountains. Its name comes from alma, the Kazakh word for apple, and the naming was not sentimental. The slopes above the city carry forests of wild apple trees that have grown there without human planting for millions of years. Travellers in the nineteenth century described riding for days through woodland where every second tree bore fruit, and where the apples underfoot were so numerous that the air smelled of cider by late summer.
BThe tree responsible is Malus sieversii. What sets it apart from other wild apple species is size. Most wild apples are small, hard and bitter — the European crab apple rarely exceeds three centimetres across. M. sieversii, by contrast, regularly produces fruit as large as a modern supermarket apple, and individual trees in the Tian Shan vary enormously: some bear fruit that is sweet and yellow, others sour and deep red, others small and astringent. This variation, spread across a single wild population, is far greater than anything found in commercial orchards.
CWhy the fruit grew so large is a question of who was eating it. Small, hard apples are dispersed by birds, which swallow seeds whole and pass them intact. But the Tian Shan forests were also home to bears, deer and wild horses. A bear approaching a tree does not eat indiscriminately; it works through the fruit and returns to the trees whose apples are largest and sweetest. Seeds from those trees travelled furthest, in a package of fertiliser, and were more likely to establish. Over a very long period, the animals were performing a slow, unplanned selection — and the result was a wild fruit already halfway to domestication before any human touched it.
DFrom the second century BCE, the trade routes later known collectively as the Silk Road ran directly through this region. Apples travel badly but apple seeds travel well: they are small, dry and remain viable for months. Seeds moved west with caravans, were discarded at campsites, and germinated along the roadside. Where those trees met the native European crab apple, Malus sylvestris, the two hybridised freely. Genetic work published in recent decades has confirmed that the modern domesticated apple carries substantial contributions from both species, with the Central Asian ancestor dominant and the European one adding hardiness and disease resistance.
EThere was, however, an obstacle that no amount of seed-carrying could solve. Apples are extreme outbreeders: a seed from a sweet apple will almost never produce a tree bearing sweet fruit. Plant a hundred pips from one prized tree and you may get a hundred trees, all different, and most of them disappointing. Every named variety in existence — every Fuji, every Golden Delicious — is therefore not a lineage but a single original tree, kept alive by grafting cuttings onto rootstock, over and over, sometimes for centuries. Grafting was already an established horticultural technique in the ancient world, and it is what turned the apple from a lottery into a crop.
FThe wild forests that began the whole process are now among the most threatened woodland in Central Asia. Estimates suggest that a large majority of the original M. sieversii forest has disappeared over the past century, cleared for orchards and housing as Almaty expanded up the slopes, and thinned by livestock which eat the seedlings before they can establish. The irony is sharp. Commercial apple growing worldwide now rests on a very narrow genetic base, and when breeders need resistance to a new disease, the place they increasingly look is the Tian Shan — the forest that is being cut down to plant the orchards that need it.
